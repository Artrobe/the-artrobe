The hardest part of a workshop is the first ten minutes. A room of adults who have not made anything with their hands since school, most of them convinced within minutes that they are the least capable person present. Someone always says it out loud: *I'm not creative.*

They are wrong, but arguing does not help. The only thing that works is making the first step so small that scepticism has nothing to hold onto.

## Design the session backwards

Every workshop is built from the finish line. What are people carrying out of the room? It has to be complete, it has to be theirs, and it has to survive the car journey home.

That last constraint sounds trivial and is not. Wet media is out. Anything needing overnight drying is out. The piece has to be dry, framed by its own edges, and robust enough to be handed around an office the next morning.

Working backwards from that, the technique picks itself. Texture on a rigid round base dries fast, tolerates unevenness, and looks deliberate even when it is not.

## Uneven is the point

The best thing about texture work in a group is that it cannot be done badly in an obvious way. A wobbly line in a portrait is a wobbly line. A wobbly ridge in a wave is just a wave.

This is not a trick to flatter people. It is choosing a medium whose natural variation matches the range of skill in the room. Everyone's piece looks different, nobody's looks wrong, and the differences read as personality rather than error.

## Where the shift happens

Somewhere around the fifty-minute mark, the room changes. Conversation drops. People stop asking whether they are doing it right and start making decisions — moving a shell, adding another ridge, mixing a colour nobody suggested.

That is the entire point of the session, and it cannot be scheduled. You can only build the conditions and wait for it.

## Corporate rooms are different

A team event carries something an open workshop does not: everyone knows each other, and hierarchy walks in with them. Senior people are often the most reluctant, because being visibly bad at something in front of colleagues is a real cost.

The fix is simple and slightly unfair — start everyone at the same disadvantage. Gloves on, first layer of paste down, nobody's hands clean. Once the room is equally committed, rank stops mattering.

## What they take home

The piece, obviously. But the more durable thing is the small correction to *I'm not creative* — which was never about art in the first place.
